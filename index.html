A pattern language · 24 patterns

Houses for One Night

For the buildings that are found standing at dusk, finished and humming, and are gone by morning. From the town down to the ring of dry ground it leaves.

19:00condensing

No one has watched one of these buildings being made. They are found complete, usually in the hour after sunset, in towns of a dozen to a few hundred. They stand one night. At first light they go, the way frost goes off a window: in order, from the edges in.

This language treats them as one would treat any vernacular. Larger patterns make room for smaller ones, and each pattern names the ones that complete it. It is written to the builder, as if you were the one making them. We are not. But writing it this way is the only way we have found to make the buildings make sense.

On the asterisks
**
True of every such building we have found. We believe it is part of what they are.
*
Holds in most places, but we have seen exceptions.
 
We record it because it keeps appearing. We do not understand it.

Part I

Where they come down

The town as a whole: how long it is for, where it stands, and how its buildings keep their distance.

1

A Town for One Night**

This pattern begins the language. Every pattern after it assumes the lifespan it sets.

❖❖❖

A settlement meant to last is laid out for its own survival. A settlement that knows it will be gone by morning has to be laid out for something else.

The towns are never found half-built, and never half-gone by day. Each stands complete for one night and is planned as though that night were the whole of its purpose. The main street bends toward the point where the moon will stand highest. No building is set where it would still be in shadow at dawn. Nothing is stored: there are no cellars, no granaries, no rooms for keeping things. The plan is a plan for a single use, like a table laid for one meal.

Therefore:Lay the town out for the length of one night. Turn its main street toward the moon's highest point, and give it nothing that implies a tomorrow: no stores, no foundations, no rooms for keeping.

❖❖❖

Within the town, leave one site open for the empty lot that waits (3). Set the buildings along growing toward the tide (4) and space them by distance by sound (5).

2

Arrival Before the Roads*

…once a town for one night (1) is standing, there is the question of how anyone reaches it.

❖❖❖

Roads are laid so that people can arrive. Here the buildings arrive first and no one walks to them, so the roads have some other job.

The roads are only found after the town has gone: pale bands of flattened grass, sometimes glazed, running between the places where buildings stood. They connect every building to every other one, including pairs nobody would ever walk between. None of them leads in from outside. They are not routes. They are a record of whatever passed between the buildings during the night. Surveyors who have tried to walk them report arriving a little earlier than they set out.

Therefore:Lay no road toward the town. Let paths form between the buildings while they stand, and let them be what is left afterward: a map of every exchange the town had with itself.

❖❖❖

The roads are the first of the traces in what remains: a ring of dry ground (24).

3

The Empty Lot That Waits**

…every town laid out as in a town for one night (1) has one site in it where nothing is built.

❖❖❖

If every site is filled, the town is only what its builders made. Something needs a place to come to on its own.

Near the middle of every town there is a plot the same size as the others, fully bordered, with a threshold stone at its front edge and nothing behind it. The ground inside stays dry in rain. In three of the recorded towns, when surveyors returned the following evening, a building stood on the empty lot, alone, on otherwise bare ground. It was not like the others. It had no windows, and its door faced the stone.

Therefore:Leave one lot empty in the heart of the town. Border it, set a threshold stone at its edge, and build nothing on it. Keep it dry.

❖❖❖

The building that sometimes arrives here is room for the one who has not come (16) at the scale of a whole house.

4

Growing Toward the Tide*

…with the town set for a single night (1), choose where its edge lies.

❖❖❖

A building placed safely out of reach of whatever will end it has no way of knowing when to leave.

The buildings stand in rows along a line that something is about to cross: the low-tide mark on a beach, the edge of a snowfield on a thawing night, the shadow of a hill that the sun will lift off at dawn. The front row always stands nearest that line. Each row behind it is built a little more slowly and a little more carefully, as if it had been told how long it had. The water does not destroy the town. It keeps its time.

Therefore:Put the town's front edge on the line of whatever will next arrive: tide, thaw, or sunlight. Build each row back from it for a slightly longer stay.

❖❖❖

Each building's own ending follows half-life of a roof (9) and the course of unlaying (23).

5

Distance by Sound

…within the town (1), the buildings have to be spaced.

❖❖❖

Buildings spaced by measure either crowd or scatter. These are spaced by something else, and we have only partly worked out what.

Every building hums: a low tone at the edge of hearing, different for each one. Measured on the ground, the distance between neighbours ranges from four paces to forty. Measured by ear, it never varies. Standing at any door you can just hear the next building, and you cannot hear the one beyond it. When a building leaves early, its neighbours are found to have moved.

Therefore:Space each building from the next so that its sound is just audible at the neighbour's threshold and no farther. Let quiet houses stand close and loud ones stand apart.

❖❖❖

The hum is carried by the load-bearing silence (10).

6

The Shadow Precinct

…at the edge of some towns, beyond the last row of growing toward the tide (4).

❖❖❖

A town that is complete is closed. It seems to need an edge where it can go on without being built.

Beyond the last row lies a district of shadows. They are the cast shadows of buildings, with eaves, chimneys and the outlines of open windows, lying on bare ground with nothing standing there to cast them. They move correctly with the moon. People who have stood inside one say it was colder there, and that they could hear a door.

Therefore:At the town's edge, lay out a district of shadows only. Cast them from buildings that are not there, and let them turn with the light.

❖❖❖

These shadows obey the same rule as light from the wrong side (18).

Part II

The houses

Each building: which way it faces, what holds it up, and the rate at which it lets go.

7

Buildings That Arrive Facing Away**

…once a site is given by (3), (4) and (5), each building must be turned.

❖❖❖

A building that faces the street greets whoever walks there. These buildings are not made for anyone who walks there.

Each building's principal face, the one with the ornament, the deepest eaves and the tallest door, is turned away from the street and toward something outside the town: a point in empty sky, a notch on the horizon, sometimes the empty lot. The street side is plain and nearly blank. Surveyors learn to walk around to the back of a building to find its front, and to find there that it is addressed to someone else.

Therefore:Turn each building's front away from the street, toward whatever it was built to meet. Leave the street side plain.

❖❖❖

The front door itself is the door that is only there from inside (15).

8

One Wall Left Unfinished*

…every building turned as in (7) has four walls, and only three of them are finished.

❖❖❖

A building with no way to begin its ending has to be torn down. A building that has one can simply leave.

One wall of every building is never closed. It is framed and partly filled, and its last courses stop on an uneven line, like a sentence broken off. This is the wall the building leaves by. At first light it goes first, and the rest follow. The few buildings found with all four walls closed have never been seen to go. They are the ones that remain as ruins, and nothing will live near them.

Therefore:Leave one wall unfinished, on the side the building will leave toward. Stop its courses on an uneven line.

❖❖❖

The half-wall is filled with the quickest of the materials in build with what will leave (22).

9

Half-Life of a Roof**

…each building needs a roof, and the roof must go at a rate that can be known.

❖❖❖

A roof that lasts until it suddenly fails catches whoever is under it. A roof that fades gives warning.

No roof is ever lost all at once. Measured through the night, each one loses half its substance in a fixed time, then half of what remains in the same time again. The time differs from building to building, from about an hour to most of the night, but for any one roof it never changes. So the roof never quite reaches nothing. At dawn it is a haze held at the height of the eaves, and rain falling through it comes down warm.

Therefore:Make every roof decay by halves at a steady rate suited to its building. Let the last of it hang at the height of the eaves after everything else is gone.

❖❖❖

Set the rate by the building's row in growing toward the tide (4). The roof rests on the load-bearing silence (10).

10

The Load-Bearing Silence*

…between the roof (9) and the ground.

❖❖❖

The buildings have no posts strong enough to carry what stands on them. Something else is taking the weight.

Where a house would have its main posts, these buildings have places where sound stops. Clap in the central room and the echo returns from every direction but four, arranged as columns would be. Speak into one of those places and your voice does not come back at all. When one surveyor kept up a noise at one of them for long enough, the floor above it sagged. The building's hum (5) runs around these points and never through them.

Therefore:Carry the building on columns of silence. Put them where the posts would go, keep them clear of noise, and never let the hum pass through them.

❖❖❖

One of the silences always stands near a corner that will not resolve (17). The silences outlast everything but the rings; see (22) and (24).

11

Scaffold That Outlasts the House

…around some buildings, most often the tallest.

❖❖❖

Scaffold goes up before a building and comes down after it. Here it goes up after.

On the evening after a building has gone, a scaffold is sometimes found standing in its place, lashed together from pale poles, climbing to exactly the height and outline of the vanished walls. It holds nothing up. Its planks are worn, as if a great deal of work had been done from them. It stands two or three days and then it too is gone. We believe this is how the building is taken apart. We have never seen anything on the platforms.

Therefore:Once the building has left, raise a scaffold in its exact outline, and let it stand until the unmaking is finished.

❖❖❖

This is the final stage of the course of unlaying (23).

12

A House Smaller Inside**

…inside each building made according to (7) through (10).

❖❖❖

A room large enough for a person tells that person they are welcome.

Every building is smaller inside than out. Thick walls do not explain it. Measured from both sides, the walls agree with each other, and still the rooms add up to about two-thirds of the footprint. The missing third cannot be found behind any wall. People who have spent a night in one describe the rooms as fitted, as though made for a body a little smaller than ours and much more still.

Therefore:Make the interior two-thirds the size of the exterior. Do not account for the difference.

❖❖❖

The rooms inside are given in room for the one who has not come (16) and warmth left in the chair (20). Where there is an upper floor, reach it by (13).

13

Stairs That Climb Only Once*

…where a building has more than one floor (12).

❖❖❖

A stair that can be climbed any number of times makes the floor above it ordinary.

Each stair can be climbed once. Its treads are soft underfoot, like packed snow, and they keep the print of the first foot to press them. The next person finds the stair still there but leading nowhere: the treads rise, and the landing is the floor they started from. The upper rooms are the most carefully made in the house. Almost none of us have seen them.

Therefore:Build each stair to be climbed a single time. Make the treads take a print, and after the first ascent let the stair lead back to its own foot.

❖❖❖

The rooms at the top are lit by windows onto yesterday (14).

14

Windows onto Yesterday

…in the upper rooms reached by (13), and more rarely on the ground floor.

❖❖❖

A window shows what is outside now. A building that will not see tomorrow has little use for now.

Through some windows the view is of the same place on the previous evening, before the town arrived: bare ground, standing grass. The weather in the window does not match the weather outside. Twice, a surveyor looking through one has seen themselves a day earlier, walking the empty site and taking notes. Neither remembered looking up.

Therefore:Glaze the windows of the upper rooms so that they show the site as it was before the building came.

❖❖❖

The light that comes through these windows falls as in light from the wrong side (18).

Part III

Rooms and thresholds

What the inside is for, and what it does to the people who pass through it.

15

The Door That Is Only There from Inside**

…on the principal face of every building (7).

❖❖❖

A door that opens from outside lets in whoever is outside. These doors are for leaving.

From outside, the principal face has no door. There is a carved lintel over a stretch of blank wall. From inside, the same wall has a full-height door with its latch worn smooth. Surveyors have entered through the unfinished wall (8), walked out through this door, turned around, and found only the lintel. The latches are always on the inside and always worn, though no building stands long enough to wear anything.

Therefore:Make the main door a door only from within. From outside, mark its place with a lintel and nothing else.

❖❖❖

Just before this door, place the anteroom of forgetting (19).

16

Room for the One Who Has Not Come**

…in every house made smaller inside (12), one room is set apart.

❖❖❖

A house built only for the people in it is closed to anyone else. These houses are built for someone else first.

Each building has one room better made than the rest. It has the best light, a raised sleeping shelf, and a bowl set out. The bowl is always clean and the shelf never slept on. The room is never at the centre of the plan. It lies on the side that faces the building's addressee (7), and it is the last room to go at dawn. If anyone has ever used one, they left nothing behind.

Therefore:In every house, make one room for the guest who has not arrived. Give it the best light, set out a bowl, and put it on the side facing whoever the house is waiting for.

❖❖❖

Its chair is kept as in warmth left in the chair (20). Its walls are made of the longest-lasting material (22).

17

A Corner That Will Not Resolve

…where two walls meet near one of the columns of silence (10).

❖❖❖

We do not know what problem this solves.

In one corner of each building, the walls do not meet. Seen straight on they appear to, but the eye cannot settle on the join. A plumb line hung there swings toward the wall and away and never comes to rest. Objects left in the corner are later found in the opposite one. Children, on the occasions they have been with us, would not sit with their backs to it.

Therefore:In one corner, let the walls fail to meet. Do not close the join.

❖❖❖

This corner is most often the one nearest room for the one who has not come (16).

18

Light from the Wrong Side*

…in every room lit by the windows of (14) or by lamps.

❖❖❖

Ordinary light tells you the hour and where you stand. In a house that exists for one night, neither should be trusted.

Light in the rooms falls from the side opposite its source. A lamp on the east wall throws shadows toward the east wall. Moonlight through a south window lights the south faces of things and leaves their north faces dark. After an hour inside, people start walking toward shadows to find the light, and when they leave they misjudge the way out.

Therefore:Let every source of light in the house cast its shadows toward itself.

❖❖❖

Outside the town, the same light casts the shadow precinct (6).

19

The Anteroom of Forgetting**

…between the rooms and the door that is only there from inside (15).

❖❖❖

A person leaving a house carries the house away with them. These houses do not want to be carried.

Before the door there is a small bare room, just large enough to turn around in. People who pass through it remember the building as a whole, its size, its hum and which way it faced, but not one of its rooms. Notes written inside survive and are in the writer's hand, and the writer does not recognise them. This is why surveys of these buildings are kept in two columns: what was seen, and what was later read.

Therefore:Put a small bare room between the house and the way out. Let it keep the rooms and let the house go.

❖❖❖

What is not forgotten is what the ground keeps, in what remains: a ring of dry ground (24).

20

Warmth Left in the Chair*

…in the room for the one who has not come (16), and in some of the others.

❖❖❖

An empty chair looks unused. These chairs must look as though someone has just stood up.

In each room at least one seat is warm. The warmth is exactly that of a body and has the shape of one, somewhat narrower than ours. It does not cool through the night. The cushion is pressed. If you sit down in it, the warmth moves to the next chair.

Therefore:Keep one seat in each room warm, as if just left, and let the warmth move away from anyone who takes it.

❖❖❖

This warmth is the last thing to leave; see the course of unlaying (23).

Part IV

Making and unmaking

What the buildings are made of, and the order in which they give it back.

21

Materials That Remember Being Weather**

…every pattern above is built out of something.

❖❖❖

Material taken from the ground wants to stay. These buildings need material that wants to go back.

The walls are grown from condensation: the dew of the site, the fog that lay there the evening before, the breath of anyone nearby. Scraped with a blade, a wall gives up a fine frost that melts in the hand. Its grain matches the frost that formed on the site's grass the winter before. In fog country the buildings are tall and fine. In desert they are low and thick, and they go very quickly.

Therefore:Build from the site's own weather: dew, fog, frost and breath. Let the wetter places have the finer houses.

❖❖❖

Place the materials as in build with what will leave (22).

22

Build with What Will Leave*

…having chosen materials that remember being weather (21).

❖❖❖

A structure with its strongest material at the base is built to last. A structure meant to leave needs its most lasting material wherever it must go last.

Material is placed by how long it lasts, not by how much it carries. The slowest frost, hoar, lines the room for the one who has not come (16) and gathers around the warm chairs (20). The quickest fog fills the walls nearest the tide (4) and the unfinished wall (8). The load goes to the silences (10), which do not dissolve at all and stand, unseen, for months.

Therefore:Place each material by how long it will last, with the most lasting where the building must go last. Carry the weight on silence.

❖❖❖

The order of leaving is given in the course of unlaying (23).

23

The Course of Unlaying**

…every pattern in this language ends here.

❖❖❖

A building that falls apart at random leaves wreckage. A building that unlays in order leaves only what it means to.

At first light the buildings go in a fixed sequence. First the unfinished wall (8), then the plain walls on the street side, then the last haze of the roof (9), then the stairs, the windows and the ordinary rooms. The room for the one who has not come goes last but one. The very last is the warmth in the chair (20), which can be felt in the open air for several minutes after the chair has gone. After that, where there is one, comes the scaffold (11).

Therefore:Unlay the house in reverse order of its importance: the plainest parts first, the guest's room last but one, and the warmth last of all.

❖❖❖

What is left is what remains: a ring of dry ground (24).

24

What Remains: A Ring of Dry Ground**

…this pattern completes the language.

❖❖❖

Something should be left behind, so the place knows it was chosen.

Where each building stood, the ground stays dry in a ring the width of its walls, even through rain, for about a year. Inside the ring the grass comes back first. The roads (2) fade within a week, the scaffold (11) in three days, the silences (10) over some months. The rings last longest. From the air an old site looks like a field of fairy rings, and each ring hums very faintly at its own pitch, set from its neighbour at exactly the distance where it can just be heard.

Therefore:When everything else has gone, leave a ring of dry ground where the walls stood. Leave nothing else.

❖❖❖

Where the rings lie thickest, a town for one night (1) is more likely to come down again.

Twenty-four patterns. The language is incomplete: there are certainly patterns for the upper rooms that no one has been able to write down. Surveys continue each autumn, when the fog is heaviest and the towns are most frequent.
